A. OVERALL REALISM
PARTLY. The simulation captures key structural elements of academic integrity, such as the distinction between different types of plagiarism and the role of detection probabilities, which aligns with the general definition of plagiarism as using another's work as one's own. However, the assumption that students act as rational agents maximizing cumulative rewards via Q-learning oversimplifies the complex ethical, social, and psychological factors that drive real-world academic dishonesty. While the model provides a controlled environment to test intervention strategies, it lacks the nuanced behavioral variability typical of human decision-making in academic settings.

B. TOP 3 REALISM GAPS
1. **Oversimplified Agent Decision-Making**: The use of Q-learning implies students make decisions purely to maximize cumulative reward, ignoring non-reward factors like ethical beliefs, fear of social stigma, or varying levels of moral development. This is a hypothesis needing more information, as the description does not specify if any ethical constraints or heterogeneous agent types are included beyond the reward/penalty mechanism.
2. **Static Detection Probabilities**: The model assigns fixed detection probabilities (0.55 for direct copying, 0.25 for AI-assisted). In reality, detection efficacy depends on the specific assignment, the sophistication of the plagiarism, and the resources available to educators. This is directly supported by the description, which lists these as static parameters, but it lacks evidence of dynamic adjustment based on context or evolving AI capabilities.
3. **Homogeneous Peer Network Influence**: The description states students interact through "local peer networks" but does not specify how this interaction influences decisions. It is a hypothesis needing more information whether peer pressure, social norms, or information sharing about detection rates are modeled, or if "interaction" is merely topological without behavioral impact.

C. ONE MINIMUM REVISION
Introduce heterogeneity in agent ethical thresholds by allowing a subset of agents to have a non-zero intrinsic penalty for dishonesty regardless of detection risk. To evaluate this change, compare the prevalence of AI-assisted plagiarism in the heterogeneous model against the homogeneous baseline; if the heterogeneous model shows a more realistic decay in dishonest behavior over time or a stronger response to social norms, it indicates improved behavioral realism.
